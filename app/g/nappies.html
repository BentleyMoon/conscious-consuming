Nappies: price per nappy, independent certification, and waste

A baby in disposable nappies (diapers, in the US) goes through them every day for years, so a few cents per change adds up, and every one ends in the bin. Three things can be checked from public pages: what each nappy costs, whether an outside body has certified its chemistry or materials, and where it goes afterwards. This guide covers disposables. If you are weighing cloth, the reusable nappies guide covers that decision.

The short answer. On Target's own listings, the Size 4 store brand costs about half as much per nappy as Pampers or Huggies, and neither national brand lists any third-party certification there. If independent chemical testing matters to you, look for a named mark such as OEKO-TEX Standard 100 or EWG Verified, or an ecolabel such as the Nordic Swan or EU Ecolabel, and expect to pay more per change. No brand changes the waste: EPA found no significant recycling of US disposable diapers. If a rash or reaction worries you, ask a health professional, not a label.

Independent certification. Target's specifications field says "No Certifications" for Pampers, Huggies and Luvs. For up & up it says "Meets ASTM Standards, Meets OSHA Requirements", which are general standards, not an ecolabel. Millie Moon's listing gives an OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate number you can look up. Kudos and ATTITUDE state OEKO-TEX Standard 100 on their own pages; Coterie and ATTITUDE state EWG Verified. Bambo Nature lists the Nordic Swan and EU Ecolabel, and the Swedish ecolabel administrator's own EU Ecolabel list shows 10 Bambo Nature products among 27 certified nappies.

Pulp and bleaching. Kudos, Coterie and ATTITUDE say their pulp is totally chlorine free (TCF), and Kudos and ATTITUDE say it is FSC certified. Elemental chlorine free (ECF) is a different bleaching standard, so read which one a pack claims.

Why the chemistry question is real. France's health and safety agency ANSES assessed tests run between 2016 and 2018 on nappies sold in France, without naming brands, and found some substances above its safety thresholds, including two fragrances and some dioxins and PAHs. It asked makers to stop using fragrances. That finding says nothing about any single product on sale today, which is why a certificate you can check carries more weight than "free of" wording on the box.

Waste. EPA estimates the US generated 4.1 million tons of disposable diaper waste in 2018, 1.4 percent of all municipal solid waste. About 3.3 million tons went to landfill, and EPA found no significant recycling or composting.

When money is the problem

The National Diaper Bank Network says to call 2-1-1 to find diaper help nearby, or to check its member directory. It also notes that SNAP and WIC cannot be used to buy diapers. A nationally representative 2021 survey, published in Heliyon, found 44 percent of caregivers reported diaper need, and concluded that diaper banks are unlikely to be enough given how few families they reach. Ask anyway, and keep other routes open.

Reading the pack in 30 seconds

  1. Price per nappy, at the same size. Target shows a per-count price; elsewhere, divide.
  2. A named certificate. Look in the specifications, not the slogan, and look up the number if one is given.
  3. "Free of" lists are claims. Useful, but self-declared.
  4. Pulp. FSC certified? TCF or ECF?

What we're not telling you

We do not score fit, leaks or absorbency; those depend on your baby. Prices are US and dated, and they move. If your baby has a rash that does not settle, or you suspect a reaction to a nappy, talk to your health visitor, doctor, pediatrician or pharmacist.
