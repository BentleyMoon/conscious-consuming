Used cars: price, buyer protection, and open recalls

The same used car can be a good buy or a costly mistake depending on who sells it to you and what they promise in writing. This guide compares the routes buyers in the United States use, from a neighbor's driveway to a certified dealer lot to an auction, because the route decides how much protection comes with the car.

The short answer. Price and protection pull against each other. A private sale is usually cheapest and carries almost no protection. A maker's certified pre-owned car costs a little more and comes inspected with a factory-backed warranty. Big retailers sell at fixed prices with return windows. Buy-here-pay-here lots lend to people banks turn down, at a steep price. Whatever the route, check the VIN for open recalls, read the Buyers Guide, and pay for an independent inspection. Keeping the car you already have running is often the cheapest option of all.

Weigh what you care about

Price. iSeeCars says you "will almost always pay more for a vehicle from a dealer than you would with a private seller," because dealer prices carry overhead, salaries and profit. Consumer Reports found certified pre-owned cars cost about 1.8 percent more than comparable non-certified ones.

Protection. Consumer Reports' analysis of more than 300,000 vehicles found certified pre-owned cars had about 14 percent fewer problems than other used cars. It also warns that some dealers "certify" cars themselves or sell third-party certifications, and that "these types of certified pre-owned programs have risks." Ask whose program it is.

At the other end, a private sale has no company behind it. Facebook's Purchase Protection does not cover local pickups or Messenger deals. Minnesota's Attorney General warns about curbstoners, unlicensed dealers posing as private sellers, who "may not hold title to the vehicles they sell" and may roll back odometers or sell wrecked, salvaged or stolen cars. Ask to see the title and check the seller's name is on it. eBay Motors is the exception among marketplaces: its Vehicle Purchase Protection, included at no charge on eligible purchases, covers non-delivery, title problems and undisclosed major damage, up to $500,000 per transaction completed from 1 August 2026.

Open recalls. A Chicago Sun-Times investigation in 2023 found used trucks with unrepaired safety recalls listed for sale at AutoNation, and noted there is no federal law stopping dealers selling used cars with open recalls. NHTSA's lookup shows whether a specific car has an unrepaired recall when you enter its 17-character VIN or plate. It does not show recalls more than 15 years old, and some recent recalls may not yet list every VIN, so check again close to the sale.

Financing. A Federal Reserve note from May 2026 found buy-here-pay-here loans carried an average rate of 25.39 percent against 14.60 percent for traditional lenders' subprime loans, and that about 5 percent of their balances were in active repossession in the third quarter of 2025, against less than half a percent for traditional lenders. The FTC advises you to compare the total cost with financing, not only the monthly payment.

Big online retailers. Connecticut's Attorney General settled with Carvana for $1.5 million in January 2025 after complaints about months-long title and registration delays. The settlement requires valid title and registration documents at the time of sale.

There is no single best route. Someone with cash and a trusted mechanic can do well privately; someone who needs a warranty and a return window may be wiser paying more.

Checks for any route

The FTC says dealers must display a Buyers Guide on every used car, stating whether it is sold "as is" or with a warranty. It adds that federal law does not give you three days to cancel, so get any return policy in writing. A vehicle history report is useful but "not a substitute for an independent vehicle inspection." If a dealer will not allow one, the FTC suggests going to another dealer.

If you are leasing now, Experian notes your buyout price, the residual value, is listed in your lease contract; compare it with the car's current market value before deciding.

What we're not telling you

We do not rate car models, fuel economy, emissions or reliability by make. Price patterns are averages, not a quote for any car. Safety is for NHTSA, your state's consumer protection office and a qualified mechanic to judge; we give no mechanical, legal or financial advice.
