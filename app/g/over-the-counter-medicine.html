Over-the-counter medicine: active ingredient, price per tablet, and the label

At Target, a 100-count bottle of Tylenol Extra Strength costs $10.99. Target's own up & up acetaminophen, with the same 500 mg of the same active ingredient per caplet, costs $1.99. That gap repeats across the medicine cabinet, and it is the one part of this decision you can check without any medical knowledge. Which medicine suits you, and how much to take, is for the label and a pharmacist.

The short answer. Compare the first line of the Drug Facts label, the active ingredient and its amount per tablet. Where they match, the store brand cost roughly a fifth to a third of the national brand in every pair we checked. FDA says generic medicines are made to be the same as the brand in strength, dosage form, safety and intended use, though inactive ingredients can differ and, in a minority of people, may affect tolerability. The warnings travel with the ingredient, so a cheaper box carries the same ones. Before buying at all, check what you already own.

Why matching ingredients matter

Generic equivalence. FDA defines a generic as a medicine made to be the same as the brand in dosage form, safety, strength, route, quality, performance and intended use, and says generics are expected to have the same clinical effect. Colour can differ. FDA also notes that inactive ingredients can, in a minority of patients, affect tolerability or absorption, so if you react to a dye or filler, show both labels to a pharmacist.

How each product is authorised. DailyMed, the US National Library of Medicine's label database, shows it. Target's ibuprofen label lists an approved generic application (ANDA). Its acetaminophen is listed under an OTC monograph, which FDA has described, GAO reports, as a "rulebook" setting active ingredients, uses, dosage forms and labelling for products such as aspirin and cough medicine. Either way, the Drug Facts label must list the active ingredient and its amount first.

The warnings are the same. Target's acetaminophen label on DailyMed carries the same severe liver damage warning as Tylenol Extra Strength, including the risk of taking it with other products that contain acetaminophen. Advil's and Target's ibuprofen labels both carry the NSAID warnings about stomach bleeding and heart attack and stroke. Benadryl's label says "marked drowsiness may occur"; Zyrtec's says "drowsiness may occur". Read them on the pack every time.

Check the cabinet first

FDA warns that many OTC medicines have similar brand names with different active ingredients, and the reverse happens too: the same ingredient under different names. Before you buy, read the active ingredient on what you already have. You may already own it, and doubling up is what the acetaminophen and ibuprofen labels warn against. For a mild fever, Cleveland Clinic's page explains when medicine is usually not needed and when to call a provider.

What we're not telling you

We do not say which medicine to take, how much, or for how long. Pregnancy, children, older age, liver, kidney, heart or stomach conditions, and other medicines all change what is safe, and the label's "Ask a doctor or pharmacist" lines exist for that.
