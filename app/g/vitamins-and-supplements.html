Vitamins and supplements: independent testing, regulator records, and price

This page will not tell you whether a vitamin works, or whether you need one. That is a question about your body, your diet and your medicines, and it belongs with a doctor or pharmacist. What this page can do is help you compare the things about a bottle that can be checked from outside: whether an independent tester has verified it, what the regulator has on record, and what it costs.

The short answer. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before you take anything, and tell them about every supplement you already take, because some interact with medicines. If you do buy, look for a product that appears in an independent register such as USP Verified or NSF Certified for Sport, check FDA's warning letters and health fraud list for the brand, and compare the price per tablet. The NHS says most people get the nutrients they need from a varied and balanced diet, which costs nothing extra.

Start with a professional, and the free option

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements tells you to consult your health care provider before taking supplements to treat a health condition, and to get their approval before taking them in place of, or together with, prescribed medicines. It warns that supplements can interact with some medicines, and asks for caution in pregnancy, while nursing, and when giving supplements to children (NIH ODS). The NHS says most people should get all the nutrients they need from a varied and balanced diet, although some people may need supplements (NHS). Which group you are in is the professional's call.

Why independent testing matters here

The ODS says medicines must be approved by the FDA before they are sold, while supplements do not need that approval, and supplement companies are responsible for having evidence that their products are safe and their labels truthful (NIH ODS). The FDA says it can act against an adulterated or misbranded supplement after it reaches the market (FDA).

An independent seal fills part of that gap. USP says its Verified Mark means the product contains the listed ingredients in the declared amounts, does not contain harmful levels of specified contaminants such as heavy metals and pesticides, was made under FDA Good Manufacturing Practices, and will break down in a set time (USP). NSF Certified for Sport says it tests for more than 280 substances banned in sport (NSF).

Two limits apply. The ODS says these seals "do not guarantee that a product is safe or effective." And a seal belongs to a product, never to a whole brand, so check the exact bottle.

What the registers show

On the day we checked, USP's register listed Nature's Bounty Vitamin D3 125 mcg Softgels, Kirkland Signature Daily Multi Tablets, and, on one page of its list, vitafusion MultiVites Gummies and Ritual Essential for Women Multivitamin 18+ (USP register). A search of the register for Centrum returned no results (USP register, Centrum). NSF's Certified for Sport search lists Thorne's Multi-Vitamin Elite (NSF, Thorne).

A product missing from a register may simply never have applied. It is unverified by that register, which is a different thing from unsafe.

Check the regulator's record

We have not yet checked each brand in the explorer against FDA enforcement, so here is how you can. FDA lists warning letters for food, beverages and dietary supplements, with an archive back to 1996 (FDA warning letters). Its Health Fraud Product Database collects products cited in warning letters, advisory letters, recalls and public notices; FDA says the list is "only a small fraction" of potentially hazardous products, so absence proves little. Advertising claims fall to the Federal Trade Commission, the ODS notes. Search the brand name in both places before you buy, and be wary of any label that promises to treat or cure a disease.

Price per tablet

Divide the price by the count. On the day we checked: Equate Complete Multivitamin Adults 50+, 125 tablets for $5.12 on Walmart's Instacart storefront, about 4 cents each (Instacart); Kirkland Signature Daily Multi, 500 tablets from $19.89 on Costco's Instacart storefront, also about 4 cents (Instacart); Thorne Multi-Vitamin Elite, 180 capsules for $76, about 42 cents (Optum Store); and Ritual Essential for Women, a 30-day supply of two capsules a day for $37.50 plus $10.99 shipping as a one-time order (Ritual).

A higher price does not by itself mean independent verification: Kirkland's multi is in USP's register at about 4 cents a tablet.

A five-minute check

  1. Ask a pharmacist or doctor whether you need it, and whether it interacts with anything you take.
  2. Search the exact product in USP's and NSF's registers.
  3. Search the brand in FDA's warning letters and Health Fraud Product Database.
  4. Work out the price per tablet.

What we are not telling you

We do not judge whether any vitamin or mineral works, what dose you need, or whether one brand is safer than another. None of this is medical advice. If you think a supplement has made you unwell, the ODS says to tell your health care provider.
