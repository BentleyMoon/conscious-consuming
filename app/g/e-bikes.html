E-bikes: battery safety, price, and where you buy

An e-bike carries a large lithium-ion battery, and that battery is the first thing to judge. Motor power, range and looks come after. Whether the battery and electrical system were tested against a recognized fire-safety standard depends mostly on where you buy, so our explorer compares routes rather than models.

The short answer. In the US, look for an e-bike whose electrical system is certified to UL 2849, and a battery certified to UL 2271, from a seller who can show you the certificate. In the UK, buy from a known seller and avoid fitting a conversion kit yourself. The cheapest routes, an unfamiliar online import or a home conversion, are the ones fire services and regulators warn about. If you would rather not keep a battery at home at all, bike share and e-bike lending libraries exist in some places.

Why the battery comes first

The UK's Office for Product Safety and Standards says e-bike lithium-ion batteries "can present a risk of serious fire or explosion in certain circumstances." In a New York City Council release from October 2024, a state assemblymember put the previous year's toll at 268 battery fires and 18 lives. The city's Local Law 39 requires e-bikes sold, rented or leased secondhand there to be certified to UL 2849, and batteries to UL 2271. By October 2024, the city's consumer protection department had sent 40 cease-and-desist letters and issued more than 25 violations to online retailers, on top of more than 650 inspections of shops.

Consumer Reports strongly recommends "considering only bikes (and replacement batteries) that have been tested by Underwriters Laboratory (UL) or another nationally recognized laboratory."

The routes, one at a time

A local bike shop. Consumer Reports says most bike shops sell e-bikes and their staff "are typically quite knowledgeable about them." That is where you can ask to see a certification before you pay.

A brand that publishes its certification. Rad Power Bikes, for example, lists which of its models have batteries and electrical systems certified to UL 2849 and 2271. A published list is something you can check; a vague "meets safety standards" is not.

Certified used. Trek's Red Barn Refresh accepts Trek e-bikes under two years old and checks service bulletins and recalls as part of a 151-point inspection. Upway, a used e-bike marketplace, runs a 50-point inspection, refurbishes or replaces batteries below 80 percent of their original capacity, and gives a one-year warranty against mechanical and electrical defects.

A big-box store. Consumer Reports says the salespeople "are seldom bicycle experts," so there may be no one on the floor who can confirm a certification.

A conversion kit. London Fire Brigade warns that kit batteries and chargers are sometimes bought from different sellers, which "increases the risk of incompatible equipment being used," that batteries bought online "may not meet UK safety regulations and are more likely to fail," and that DIY installation can damage the kit. Its advice: get a professional to carry out any conversion.

A private sale. The UK government's advice is to "only BUY an e-bike, e-scooter, charger or battery from a known seller." A stranger's battery comes with no record of how it was charged, stored or dropped.

Not owning one. Citi Bike members pay $0.27 a minute extra to ride an e-bike, capped at $5.40 for rides of 45 minutes or less that enter or leave Manhattan. Portland State University researchers counted 54 e-bike lending libraries in the US, most funded by grants.

There is no single best e-bike route. The safest routes cost more or reach fewer people, and the cheapest carry the risk regulators name.

Rules, recalls and people to ask

In Great Britain, an e-bike counts as an electrically assisted pedal cycle only if its motor is rated at no more than 250 watts and stops helping above 15.5 mph; riders must be 14 or over. A bike outside those limits is treated as a moped or motorcycle, with registration, tax, license and insurance. Other countries and US states set their own rules.

Before buying, search the recall databases: the Consumer Product Safety Commission in the US and the Office for Product Safety and Standards in the UK. The UK advice is to charge in a safe place without blocking exits and to unplug when charging is finished. For the bike itself, ask a qualified mechanic. We give no mechanical or legal advice here.

What we're not telling you

We do not rate motors, range or ride quality, and we do not score how share schemes maintain their fleet batteries, because no source we read covers it. Certification lowers risk; it does not remove it.
