Vet services: who owns the practice, whether it says so, and how to reach care

Most people pick a vet by distance, a friend's recommendation, or who can see the animal this week. Ownership rarely comes up, yet it has moved quickly: a 2022 statement by the chair of the US Federal Trade Commission cited an estimate that corporate consolidators had reached close to half of all companion animal practice revenue. This guide covers who owns what, how to find out, and what you can ask for at any practice. It cannot tell you how good any clinic's care is.

The short answer. Ownership is checkable, and some chains state it plainly: Banfield says it joined Mars in 2007, VCA says it joined Mars in 2017, Petco's Vetco Total Care hospitals sit inside Petco stores, and Chewy's clinics carry the Chewy name. Others do not: Thrive's homepage does not name its investor, TSG Consumer Partners, and AmeriVet's about page does not name AEA Investors. Wherever you go, you can ask for a written prescription and fill it elsewhere, and you can see whether your state's veterinary board offers a license lookup, as California's does. In an emergency, go to the nearest emergency vet; ownership questions can wait.

Saying so. Banfield's about page states the Mars link, and VCA's says it "joined the Mars family in 2017". Thrive's homepage says it is "backed by a community of veterinary experts" and names no investor. AmeriVet's about page names no owner either.

Why regulators care. In 2020 the FTC required NVA and Compassion-First to sell three clinics as a condition of merging. In 2022, reviewing a further JAB deal that would have put nearly 100 specialty and emergency clinics under one owner, FTC Chair Lina Khan called the pattern "stealth roll-ups" and warned that a focus on short-term profits in health care can "reduce quality of care, increase costs". The proposed order added a 10-year requirement for FTC approval before JAB buys specialty or emergency clinics within 25 miles of its own in California or Texas. That is the regulator's concern about a pattern, not a finding about any one clinic.

What you can ask for anywhere

  • A written prescription. AVMA policy says vets "shall honor client requests to prescribe rather than dispense a drug", and that you can fill a prescription at any pharmacy. That lets you compare medicine prices.
  • A written estimate before planned treatment, so you can compare and plan.
  • Who owns the practice. A plain question. If the answer is vague, the table above shows where to look.
  • A license check. Some state boards publish this. California's Veterinary Medical Board, for example, lets you verify a license, see disciplinary actions and file a complaint.

Other ways in

Video vets. Dutch offers 10-minute video calls and says over 90 percent of cases can be treated virtually. It says emergencies, wounds needing stitches, and anything needing blood work, imaging or surgery go to an in-person clinic.

Teaching hospitals. Veterinary colleges such as Cornell's run teaching hospitals, and AVMA publishes the official list of accredited colleges. Cornell's takes referrals from other vets, offers scheduled appointments, and runs emergency service around the clock.

There is no single best kind of vet. A chain may be the only practice with an opening this week, and an independent practice may not offer the hours or specialists you need.

What we're not telling you

We do not rate quality of care, prices or staff, and ownership changes faster than any table. Location counts are dated to their sources. Records are US only. Nothing here is veterinary advice: your vet, or an emergency vet if it cannot wait, is the right person for your animal.
