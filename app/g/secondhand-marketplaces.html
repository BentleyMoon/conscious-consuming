Secondhand marketplaces: fees, buyer protection, and local pickup

A sofa, a bread maker, a box of tools: things too big or too ordinary for a clothing app still have buyers, and often neighbors who would happily collect them for free. Most of the choice comes down to one question. Will the item travel by post, or will someone come to the door? Shipped sales put a platform in the middle to hold the money and settle disputes. Local sales and gifts mostly leave that to you.

This guide covers household goods and general items. For clothing apps, consignment and thrift, see the secondhand and resale guide.

The short answer. For things that ship, eBay and Mercari hold the payment and state a buyer guarantee, and you pay for that: eBay's final value fee is 13.6% in most categories, Mercari's selling fee is 10% and its buyers pay a 3.6% protection fee. For things that do not ship, local classifieds are free to post in most categories, and the protection is whatever you check at the handoff: Craigslist's terms say it makes no promises and caps its liability at $100. If you only want something gone, or need something free, Freecycle, the Buy Nothing Project and, in the UK, Freegle cost nothing on either side. Habitat for Humanity ReStores and Salvation Army thrift stores take donations and sell in person, and the money goes to their programs. There is no single best route.

Seller fees at zero usually mean the cost moved to the buyer, as with Vinted and Gumtree, or that nobody is standing behind the sale, as with Craigslist.

Local handoffs

Craigslist's terms say the service is provided "AS IS" and that its liability will not exceed $100 or what you paid it in the prior year. Its safety page asks you to meet somewhere public, such as a bank or a police department, not to invite strangers into your home, to be especially careful with high-value items, and to tell someone where you are going. Some police departments set aside areas for exactly this. The same advice holds for any local sale.

Freecycle works the same way without money: members post what they want to give or get, reply to each other, and "arrange a pickup time and location."

Where your money goes at nonprofit stores

Habitat ReStores are run by local Habitat organizations, accept donated furniture, appliances and building materials, and sell them at "a fraction of the retail price"; the sales help Habitat build and repair homes. The Salvation Army lists 887 thrift stores in the US and says store proceeds fund its rehabilitation centers along with programs such as food pantries and after-school care.

Before you post

  1. Decide whether it ships. If it does not, go local.
  2. Price after fees, on the day you list.
  3. For a shipped sale, keep the payment inside the platform, where the guarantee applies.
  4. For a local sale, meet in public and test the item before paying.
  5. If it is worth little, give it away. A neighbor's use beats a landfill.

What we are not telling you

Protection scores describe what each platform states, not how often it pays claims. Figures are US or UK rates as each page gives them. Facebook Marketplace is left out of our data because its help pages returned errors throughout our research, so no score would have had a real source. Nextdoor's figures come from a 2021 press release: at the time it said it reached 276,000 neighborhoods and that 25% of items in its buy, sell and give section were listed free. The cheapest option is often borrowing, repairing, or asking a neighbor before buying anything at all.
