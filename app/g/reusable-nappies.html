Reusable nappies: cost of a full supply, the laundry footprint, and ease of use

Cloth nappies ask for money up front and a wash every couple of days, and give back a stash you can reuse on a second child or pass on. The systems differ a lot in how many parts they have, how much handling they need and what a full supply costs. This guide is about choosing a cloth system. If you are comparing disposables, see the disposable nappies guide.

The short answer. Price the whole supply, not one nappy: GroVia says full-time use of its one-piece nappy costs $550 to $600, and Little Lamb sells a complete UK kit at £415 on sale (£594 regular). The UK Environment Agency found that how you wash and dry cloth decides most of its climate impact. Its average scenario came out close to a disposable. Fuller loads, line drying and reuse on a second child together cut it by 40 percent; washing at 90C and tumble drying every load raised it by 75 percent. Borrow a trial kit before you buy, and check whether your council offers a voucher.

A single nappy price says little. A full-time stash needs many changes plus the extras: Little Lamb's kit, for example, bundles 15 pocket nappies, 10 fitted nappies, 5 wraps, a pail and a wet bag. That is why the two full-supply prices above are the useful ones.

Where the footprint is decided

The UK Environment Agency's 2008 life-cycle study compared the two directly. For the average 2006 disposable it found about 550kg of carbon dioxide equivalent over the two and a half years a child is typically in nappies. For shaped reusables washed and dried the average way, about 570kg. Then the spread:

  • Line drying outdoors all the time, or washing fuller loads, each cut the reusable figure by 16 percent.
  • Fuller loads, outdoor line drying and reuse on a second child together cut it by 40 percent.
  • Tumble drying everything raised it 43 percent; washing at 90C instead of 60C raised it 31 percent; both together, 75 percent.

The study's own advice to cloth users: line dry outside when you can, tumble dry as little as possible, do not wash above 60C, wash fuller loads, choose efficient machines when you replace them, and reuse nappies on other children. It is UK data from 2008 and did not test brands, so treat it as a guide to habits rather than a verdict on any system. Little Lamb's own care guidance, a full long cycle at 40 or 60 degrees and line drying in sunshine where possible, fits it.

Try, borrow, or get help paying

  • Nappy libraries. Real Nappies for London says cloth nappy libraries let you try before you buy, with kit loans by the week or month and some long-term loans. Most are run by volunteer parents.
  • Council vouchers. In 12 participating London boroughs, residents can claim a voucher worth up to £70 toward reusable nappies or a nappy laundry service. Outside London, ask your own council.
  • Secondhand. NCT runs Nearly New Sales for baby items. The study counted reuse on a second child among the changes that lower the footprint, and buying used extends a nappy's life the same way. Condition and hygiene depend on the seller.
  • A laundry service. Where one operates, the same London voucher can pay toward someone else collecting and washing the nappies.

There is no single best system. The cheapest take the most folding and fiddling, the easiest cost more per piece, and any of them can end up worse for the climate than disposables if every load goes in the tumble dryer.

What we're not telling you

We do not score fit, leaks or how a nappy holds up over years of washing. Prices are from each brand's page in September 2026 and move often. If your baby's skin reacts, or you are unsure about washing for hygiene, ask your health visitor, doctor or pediatrician.
