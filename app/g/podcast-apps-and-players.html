Podcast apps: listening privacy, portability, and staying power

The price of a podcast app is rarely the thing to compare. What differs is what the app records about your listening, whether it ties you to one company, and whether it will still exist next year. There is no single best podcast app. There are apps that fit different phones and different comfort levels with tracking.

The short answer. Podcasts run on RSS, an open format, so most shows play in most apps and you can switch whenever you like. If privacy matters most, pick an app whose own policy keeps your listening away from ad targeting, and keep an exported copy of your subscriptions so no single company holds them. If you already use an app you like, checking its ad settings takes five minutes and costs nothing.

Why the format matters

The RSS 2.0 specification is published by Harvard's Berkman Center under a Creative Commons Attribution/Share Alike licence. Apple's podcast requirements say feeds must follow RSS 2.0 and "can be self-generated and hosted." In practice, a show lives at an address its maker controls, and any player that reads RSS can fetch it.

The exception is the exclusive. iHeartRadio's own site advertises "popular and exclusive podcasts," which means some shows play only in that app.

Apps also close. Google's former podcast address now says "Google Podcasts is no longer available" and sends people to YouTube Music. Your insurance is a subscription list you can take with you. AntennaPod's backup guide shows the usual route: an OPML export moves your subscriptions to another app, though played-episode history and settings stay behind.

What each app says it does with your listening

These come from each company's own policy, read in September 2026.

  • Apple Podcasts. Apple's privacy page says listening data is linked to a random device identifier that is not stored with your Apple Account, and that podcast providers get data only in aggregate.
  • Overcast. An iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch app that is mostly free with small ads. Its policy says no personal data targets those ads except the categories of podcasts you subscribe to, such as "Technology."
  • Pocket Casts. Its policy says it does not "sell" or "share" personal data as US state privacy laws define those terms. The app is now free, and its Android source code is public under the MPL-2.0 licence.
  • AntennaPod. Its site describes an open-source Android app, built by volunteers without commercial interest, that contains no ads and subscribes to any RSS feed.
  • Spotify. Its privacy policy says it keeps streaming history for the life of an account and uses listening for tailored ads: "if you are listening to a cooking podcast, you may hear an ad for a food processor." You can switch this off in the Tailored Ads setting.
  • Podbean. Its policy says it "may sell, trade, or otherwise share your personal information" and records listener location at city or region level from IP addresses.
  • Podcast Addict. Its policy names four ad networks (AdMob, AppLovin, Fyber and InMobi) and says they may be able to guess your location from your IP address.
  • YouTube. In 2019 Google and YouTube paid $170 million to settle FTC and New York allegations that YouTube collected identifiers from viewers of child-directed channels without parental consent and used them for targeted ads.

One thing no app setting fixes: when an app fetches an episode straight from the show's server, that server sees you. Podcast Addict's policy spells it out: the podcast's author can see your IP address, device and operating system each time you update a feed, stream or download.

Five minutes with the app you already have

  1. Open its settings and look for ad personalisation or tailored ads. Turn it off if you want to.
  2. Look for an OPML export. Save the file somewhere you keep backups.
  3. Check whether any show you love is exclusive to one app.
  4. Delete podcast apps you no longer open.

What we are not telling you

We do not score sound, design or features, which are personal. Privacy scores reflect what each company's policy says, not a measurement of the data an app actually sends. Castro has no privacy score in our data yet. Policies change, so check the date on each source.
