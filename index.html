What's happening. The four steps that turn your weights into an order. These numbers change when you move the sample's sliders.

Why it matters. Most rankings you meet were ordered by someone else's priorities, usually whoever paid for the top spot. This one runs on yours, and every step of it is on the page.

Devil's advocate. The coffee is made up. Five types stand in for thousands of real bags. And an average can hide one terrible fact behind four good ones, which is why hard lines exist, and why the app caps a score when something you care about a lot scores 20 or less.

Rabbit hole. How scores are calculated, then the worked formula on the standard's page, which runs the real engine.

Other tools that use the same files

Group values
Put several values files side by side. Everyone's hard lines hold, and where you disagree stays on the page.
Shared plan
What a group decides to do. It keeps the reason for each step, and who disagreed.
Fact repair
Send a correction with its source. Once it is checked, the fact changes for everyone, and the change stays on record.
Ownership map
Who owns a brand is a fact with a receipt too, so a look-alike choice can be traced to whoever is behind it.

The apps and what they share

Values Commons is this site. It holds the open standard for values files and the tools that use those files, alone or in a group. Three apps use the same engine and the same file formats, so a values file you make in one works in the others.

The rules underneath

Your values file follows an open standard, so any app can read it and anyone can build another app on the same files. For builders: The Open Values Standard.

Bentley Moon makes it, at the Futurism Institute.

What it will not do, and how to check

The promiseHow you can check it
No ads and no pay-to-rankThe order is facts times your weights. Step 3 above is the whole formula, and the app shows it for every score.
No account and no trackingThere is no sign-in anywhere. Your values file stays on your device unless you send it to someone.
Every fact shows its sourceOpen any score in the app and each fact links the page it came from. Every release is checked for a fact without a source, and fails if it finds one.
It says when the evidence is thin343 measures are published as withheld, next to the 423 that earned a top mark.
Same inputs, same resultThe code and data are public, so anyone can rerun a ranking and compare. Source, data and audits.
Yours to keepIt works offline and fits on a flash drive. If you dislike how it is run, take a copy and run your own.

If you catch it breaking one of these, write in.

Why it matters for security

A data breach needs somewhere the data is kept. Values Commons keeps nothing about you on a server: no account, no password, no history. What you set stays in your browser.

What protects youHow you can check it
No account to break intoThere is no sign-in, so there is no password to steal. Your values file holds weights from 0 to 5 and nothing that says who you are.
Your browser blocks sending your data elsewhereEvery page comes with a security policy (the Content-Security-Policy header) that lets it connect only to this site. Open your browser's network panel, click the page, and read the connect-src line. The one exception is the barcode scanner. If a barcode is not in the app's own files, the app asks Open Food Facts about it, and the barcode is all it sends.
No code from other companiesThe app uses no outside libraries and loads no scripts from other sites, so a hacked package somewhere else cannot reach it. package.json lists no runtime dependencies, and the same policy refuses scripts from anywhere else.
Security choices, compared with sourcesThe app compares password managers, VPNs, messengers, browsers and email, each fact with its source. The ownership map shows four VPN brands that look like rivals and have one owner.

Devil's advocate. Your values file is not signed, so a file someone sends you could have been edited and you would not know. The site runs on Cloudflare, so it is up only when Cloudflare is. If you find a way past any of this, report it privately.

Run your own copy

A household, congregation or co-op can run its own copy, because the whole thing is a folder of files anyone can copy. What funding would pay for.

Further reading

Other people working on the same questions: rating what we buy, keeping our own data, and running things together.

Rating products and their claims

  • Open Food Facts. A free, open database of food products, built by volunteers. Most of the food facts on this site come from it.
  • Ethical Consumer. A UK not-for-profit that has rated companies on how they behave since 1989.
  • Green claims, European Commission. Its study found that 53% of green claims on products in the EU are vague, misleading or unfounded.

Keeping your own data

  • Local-first software, Martin Kleppmann, Adam Wiggins, Peter van Hardenberg and Mark McGranaghan (Ink & Switch, 2019). Why an app should keep your data on your own device and still let you share it. The values file here works that way.
  • The ‘Enshittification’ of TikTok, Cory Doctorow (Wired, January 2023). How platforms get worse for their users once those users cannot easily leave.
  • Solid, started by Tim Berners-Lee. People keep their data in stores they control, and apps ask to read it.

Running things together, and attention

  • Beyond Markets and States, Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize lecture, 2009). Her evidence that communities can look after shared resources themselves, without a company or a government running them.
  • Center for Humane Technology, co-founded by Tristan Harris. Work on how apps compete for people's attention, and what to do about it.

What this is

Values Commons is free, open software for choosing by your values, alone or with other people. The coffee panel above uses made-up types of coffee; the live apps rank real entries on sourced facts.
