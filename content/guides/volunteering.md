# Volunteering: how fast you can start, and what you hand over first

Giving time asks for something money does not. Before you help, you are often asked who you are: a name and an email address at one end, fingerprints and your full legal history at the other. Neither end is better. The screening usually follows how close you will be to people who could be harmed, and it exists to protect them.

> **The short answer.** Match the route to the time and the trust you can offer. For a single day, pick a role with light screening: a Habitat for Humanity build day outside four named roles, a Special Olympics day-of-event shift (a registration form and a photo ID), or a listing on Idealist or JustServe. For regular contact with children, older people or people with disabilities, expect checks and treat them as a good sign: Big Brothers Big Sisters requires a written application, background and reference checks and an in-person interview. The Peace Corps investigates every applicant and says legal clearance can take two to four months, sometimes longer. There is no single best way to volunteer.

## What each route asks before you begin

| Route | To start | Screening the organization describes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Idealist (VolunteerMatch joined it in early 2025) | A free account, then apply to listings | None at platform level; the host organization may add its own. The site lists 100,000+ ways to help across 56 cause areas |
| JustServe | A free account; run by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and open to everyone | None described at platform level |
| DoSomething | For ages 13 to 25 | None described |
| Habitat for Humanity | Join a build day | Background check only for trip team leaders, Care-A-Vanners, Disaster Corps volunteers and AmeriCorps host site managers |
| Special Olympics (Texas policy, implementing the national one) | Day-of-event: a registration form and a photo ID | Coaches, chaperones and other roles with regular close contact or trust: application, training and a criminal background check |
| National Park Service | A signed volunteer service agreement (form OF301a, or OF301b for groups) | Parks may add forms for background checks, federal identity cards or vehicle use, depending on the assignment |
| Meals on Wheels | A form, then a call to your local provider | Varies by location; you "may be asked" to undergo a background check |
| American Red Cross | Enter your zip code, apply, create a Volunteer Connection account | A background check before an Engagement Specialist contacts you to find a match |
| Big Brothers Big Sisters | Written application, interview, orientation and training | Background and reference checks for every volunteer |
| AmeriCorps | Apply to a program | For covered positions: a sex offender registry check, state criminal history checks and an FBI fingerprint check, all done by the day before service |
| Peace Corps | Application, then a conditional invitation | A background investigation of every applicant, fingerprints, and disclosure of all citations, arrests and charges, even if dismissed, sealed or expunged |

## What happens to the data you hand over

A background check means handing over your identity. Habitat for Humanity's FAQ is a useful model because it answers each question plainly. A screening company called Asurint runs the check. You provide your full legal name, date of birth, Social Security number, phone number, email and address history. It covers a national criminal check, the national sex offender registry and searches required under the U.S. Patriot Act. It does not look at your credit. Habitat pays for it, it is valid for three years, and if you take the role your screen is ordinarily kept for no more than three years.

Before you give anyone your Social Security number or fingerprints, ask the same five questions: who runs the check, what it covers, who pays, how long the result is kept, and how you can dispute it. Habitat's FAQ notes that federal law requires a chance to dispute before an adverse decision.

## If you care most about...

| Your priority | Kind of route | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting this week | A listing site or a day-of-event role | An account or a form is all it takes to begin |
| Handing over the least data | Habitat build days, Special Olympics day-of-event roles, DoSomething | No criminal history check described for these |
| Lasting impact on one person | Mentoring, such as Big Brothers Big Sisters | The screening is the price of a trusted one-on-one role |
| A structured term of national service | AmeriCorps | A defined program with its own checks |
| Service abroad | Peace Corps | Plan for months of clearance |
| A teenager getting involved | DoSomething | Built for ages 13 to 25 |

## A five-minute way to choose

1. Decide your time (one day, a few hours a month, or a term) and your contact (behind the scenes, in a group, or one-on-one).
2. Read the organization's own screening page, and ask the five data questions if a check is involved.
3. Start small. A day-of-event shift tells you whether you want the longer role.

## What we are not telling you

Our scores describe the front door, not the value of the work, and a quick sign-up does not mean the host organization will not screen you later. Local rules vary: Meals on Wheels says vetting differs by provider, and the Red Cross page we read is its Virginia FAQ on the national site. Two documents are older than the rest: Habitat's FAQ was reviewed by its legal department in October 2019, and the Special Olympics policy is a state program's document. We give no legal advice on eligibility; if your history might matter, ask the organization directly. If you would rather give money than time, see [crowdfunding platforms](#guide/crowdfunding-platforms) and [causes to support](#guide/causes-to-support).
