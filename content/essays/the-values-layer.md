# The Values Layer

*A web that ranks for you — and shows its work.*

Every day, a thousand invisible sorts decide what you see. Which search result, which product, which story, which video, which job, which restaurant rises to the top. You experience these as neutral — *the* results, *the* feed — but none of them are neutral. Each is a ranking, and every ranking answers a question: *best for whom, by what measure?* For most of the modern web, the honest answer is: **best for whoever paid, measured by a model of you that you have never seen and cannot take with you.**

This essay is about inverting that sentence.

## The thing we stopped noticing

Shoshana Zuboff gave the machine its name: *surveillance capitalism* — "the unilateral claiming of private human experience as free raw material" for behavioral data, refined into predictions, sold in markets you are not party to. The ranking that orders your world runs on a covert profile of you, built by watching you, owned by the platform, and tuned to an objective that is not yours.

It is important to say plainly why this won, because pretending it won by trickery is the first naïveté. **It won because it works and because it pays.** Engagement-ranked feeds genuinely hold attention better than the alternatives, and they convert that attention into ad revenue at planetary scale. A values-aligned web that wants to replace it cannot pretend that gravity isn't there. It has to offer something the surveillance stack structurally cannot — not more engagement, but *trust, alignment, and legibility* — and it has to be honest that the question of who pays for it is unsolved.

## What has already been tried (and what it taught us)

The good news is that we are not starting from nothing. A generation of people has been chipping at this, and the Values Layer stands on their shoulders:

- **"Protocols, not platforms"** (Mike Masnick) — move control over what people see from companies to people, by building on open protocols.
- **Middleware / algorithmic choice** (Francis Fukuyama's Stanford group; Daphne Keller's "Magic APIs") — insert a competitive, user-chosen editorial layer *between* the platform and the person.
- **A marketplace of algorithms** (Bluesky, the AT Protocol) — the most concrete version yet: replace the single master feed with thousands of custom ones. As Jay Graber put it, *"the algorithm should be something you choose, like choosing a radio station."*
- **Own your data, in spite of the cloud** (the local-first movement, Ink & Switch) and **your data in a pod you control** (Tim Berners-Lee's Solid) — portability and ownership of *data* as a first principle.
- **An open, forkable commons of facts** (Wikipedia; Open Food Facts, whose open food data already computes Nutri-Score and Eco-Score and is reused by everyone downstream).

Each of these is real, and each carries part of the answer. But notice what every one of them moves: the *algorithm* becomes selectable, or the *data* becomes portable, or the *ratings* become open — **always one piece at a time, and never the one piece that matters most.**

## The piece no one has carried

Here is the white space. In all of that work, the thing that never moves is *you* — specifically, **your values: the weights you'd put on what matters.** You can pick a feed off Bluesky's shelf, but its objective is still its builder's, not a declared artifact of yours. You can port your data under GDPR, but not your *criteria*. You can read an open rating, but not re-weight it to your own priorities.

The Values Layer carries that missing piece. Two ideas, treated as the same kind of thing — **portable, user-owned, first-class, composable:**

1. **Your values are an artifact you author and carry.** Not "bring your own algorithm" — *bring your own values.* You declare, once, what you weigh — climate, labor, privacy, cost, locality — in a profile that lives on your device and travels with you. No site rebuilds a secret model of you, because you arrive already holding the real one.

2. **Every ranking shows its work.** Each result carries its **provenance** — the sourced, dated facts behind it, and the value-weights that produced its order. Think *nutrition labels for rankings*, but going one step further than the labels researchers have already proposed: today's labels let you *read* what an algorithm did; this lets you *set the recipe* — and then shows you the result, traceable to its sources.

Put together: **middleware lets you change the radio station. The Values Layer lets the station tune itself to you — and show its work — everywhere you go.** The same value-profile, ranking food and banks and learning and news, each time transparently, each time yours.

## Four things this is not (so it stays honest)

A vision is only worth building if it can survive its own hard questions.

- **It is not decentralization for its own sake.** Moxie Marlinspike's warning stands: *people don't want to run their own servers, and never will* — decentralized systems quietly re-centralize around whatever's convenient. So portability and provenance are the goals; decentralization is only a means, used where it earns its keep. The Values Layer must work for someone who never runs anything.
- **It does not pretend the funding question is solved.** Surveillance ranking has a business model; transparent values-ranking does not, by default. Honesty here means saying so, refusing the easy money that would corrupt the ranking, and looking to the models that fund commons without ads — donations, grants, membership, public-interest patronage (Wikipedia, Consumer Reports, Open Food Facts all live this way).
- **It does not wave away the cold start.** A layer that needs both people *and* sites is a two-sided market, and at the beginning network effects work *against* you. So it does not begin by claiming the whole web. It begins in **one high-trust corner**, deep enough to be loved, and grows outward only as it earns the right.
- **It is not relativism.** "Rank by your values" does not mean all values are equal or that truth is negotiable. Value Sensitive Design (Batya Friedman) shows you can take plural values seriously *and* keep a floor: **the facts and their provenance are always truthful; the weighting is yours.** You may trade climate against cost however you like — you may not be lied to about either, and harm is not a "preference." Pluralism with provenance is, in fact, the *cure* for the filter bubble, not its cause: you can see, and try on, how someone else weighs the same honest facts.

## Where it begins

It begins where you are reading this. **Conscious Consuming** — an open, honest guide to spending by your values — is the Values Layer's first instance and its proof: one engine that already ranks food, banks, clothing, technology and causes by whatever *you* weigh, sourced and never sponsored, private by construction, running from a file on your own device. The bones underneath it — a standard for what a fact and a value and a provenance are, an engine that ranks any of them — are being written down so that the *next* corner of the web can share them. Education next, perhaps; then the slow, careful rest.

This is not a company's roadmap. It is closer to what Howard Zinn called the quiet revolution — not seized in a day, but built, patiently, by people who decided the thing in front of them should be honest. The web learned to watch us. It can learn to *serve* us instead — to rank for us, and show its work. That is the whole of it. The rest is building.
