Trust rules

  1. Name both sides. A link joins two named things, such as ExpressVPN and Kape Technologies. Never a brand and a vague "big tech".
  2. Show the source or calculation. A sourced link says who said so and in what year. A computed link says it was computed, and from what.
  3. Keep corrections visible. A challenge goes through fact repair with its own source, and once checked it changes the map for everyone.

What's happening. Four real names and three links. The two solid lines are records copied from the data file, each with its source. The dashed line is drawn only for this example, because the data has no sourced alternative for any VPN yet.

Why it matters. Switching from ExpressVPN to Private Internet Access changes the logo and not the owner. Kape also owns CyberGhost and ZenMate, so four VPN brands that look like rivals have one owner.

Devil's advocate. A receipt is only as good as its source, and these two cite Wikipedia, plus a news site for one of them, not a company filing. Both are among the 14 links in the file with a written source and no web address. Before you trust a VPN ranking, note that Kape also bought a publisher of VPN reviews, Webselenese, in 2021.

Rabbit hole. Open Kape Technologies in the app to see every brand the map links to it, or read its Wikipedia history, where it turns out Kape was called Crossrider until 2018.

A link is a small record with a source

Two names, the relation between them, a source, and the year it was recorded. A correction to a link looks the same as a correction to any other fact.

  "format": "open-values-edges",
  "version": "0.1",
  "edges": [{
    "from": "ovs:vpn/expressvpn",
    "rel": "owned-by",
    "to": "ovs:org/kape-technologies",
    "source": "Kape Technologies (British-Israeli; owner Teddy Sagi) acquired ExpressVPN in 2021; it also owns CyberGhost, Private Internet Access and ZenMate. Source: Kape Technologies (Wikipedia); CyberInsider.",
